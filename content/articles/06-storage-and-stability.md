A certificate describes material on its test date. Everything after that date is storage. Most of the variability laboratories attribute to "lot differences" is in fact accumulated degradation from handling, and the handling variables are few enough to control completely.

## The three variables

**Temperature** drives the rate of every degradation pathway. Lower is slower, with diminishing returns.

**Moisture** is the dominant one for lyophilized material. Water enables hydrolysis of the peptide backbone, and lyophilized powders are hygroscopic enough to pull it from room air. A vial opened at room temperature in a humid room takes on water immediately.

**Oxygen and light** drive oxidation, principally at methionine, cysteine and tryptophan. Cysteine-containing sequences can also form disulfide-linked dimers.

Every practice below exists to control one of these three.

## Lyophilized storage

**-20 °C in a non-frost-free freezer, desiccated, is the working standard** for most synthetic peptides, and is what most certificates specify. A frost-free freezer cycles through warming phases to prevent ice buildup; those cycles are precisely the temperature fluctuation you are trying to avoid.

**-80 °C extends shelf life further** and is worth using for long-term storage of material you will draw on for years, or for sequences known to be labile. For routine stock turned over within a year or two, the practical difference between -20 °C and -80 °C is modest compared to the difference between either and the bench.

**Desiccation matters more than the last ten degrees.** Store vials in a sealed container with desiccant. A vial at -20 °C with desiccant will generally outlast a vial at -80 °C that is opened repeatedly in a humid room.

**Short transit at ambient temperature is not a problem.** Lyophilized peptides are stable for days to weeks at room temperature as dry solids, which is why they ship without dry ice. Cold packs in a shipment are a margin of safety, not a requirement — the integrity claim rests on the material being dry and sealed, not cold.

## The single most important handling step

**Let the vial reach room temperature before you open it.**

A vial taken from -20 °C and opened immediately is a cold surface in warm air. Water condenses onto the powder within seconds, and you have just introduced the exact variable the desiccant was there to exclude. The moisture is not visible and does not come back out.

Allow 20–30 minutes on the bench, sealed, before breaking the seal. If the vial has a septum and you are inserting a needle rather than opening it, the same reasoning applies to the cold headspace.

## After reconstitution

Reconstituted peptide in solution is far less stable than the dry powder. Hydrolysis proceeds, oxidation is faster, and adsorption to container surfaces becomes relevant at low concentrations.

**Aliquot immediately.** Divide the stock into single-use volumes the moment it is dissolved. The goal is that no aliquot is ever thawed twice.

**Freeze–thaw cycles are cumulative and irreversible.** Each cycle concentrates solutes at the ice interface, exposes the molecule to a changing pH as buffer components crystallize at different rates, and mechanically stresses the solution. Three or four cycles on a labile sequence produce measurable loss. This is the most common cause of a stock solution that "stopped working" without any other change.

**Label every aliquot** with the compound, the lot, the solvent, the concentration and the date of reconstitution. Concentration derived from a gross weight without a net-content correction should be labelled as such, so a later user knows what the number means.

**Avoid repeated warming.** Thaw on ice rather than at room temperature or in the hand, and return unused solution to the freezer promptly — or discard it, which is usually the better choice for single-use aliquots.

## Solvent choice

Solubility is sequence-dependent and the certificate should state what worked. General practice:

- **Sterile water** for sequences that dissolve readily and where nothing else is needed.
- **Dilute acetic acid** for basic sequences that resist water.
- **Dilute ammonium bicarbonate or dilute ammonia** for acidic sequences.
- **DMSO** for hydrophobic sequences, usually as a concentrated stock that is then diluted into aqueous buffer. Note that DMSO is hygroscopic in its own right and that residual DMSO in the final system may itself be a variable in your assay.

Do not sonicate aggressively or heat to force dissolution; both accelerate degradation. If material resists dissolution in the solvent the certificate specifies, that is worth reporting to the supplier rather than working around.

## Practical shelf life

With correct storage — sealed, desiccated, -20 °C or below, never opened warm — lyophilized synthetic peptides are commonly stable for years. Sequences containing methionine, cysteine, tryptophan or N-terminal glutamine are more labile and deserve shorter re-test intervals and, where the work justifies it, a periodic re-assay.

The honest position for any supplier is that a shelf-life claim past the test date is an expectation, not a measurement, unless stability data exists for that specific sequence. Ask whether a stated shelf life is supported by real-time or accelerated stability testing, or whether it is a convention.

## A minimal lab SOP

1. Log the lot and the certificate reference on arrival.
2. Store sealed, desiccated, at -20 °C or below in a non-frost-free freezer.
3. Equilibrate to room temperature before opening, every time.
4. Reconstitute in the specified solvent; record solvent, concentration and date.
5. Aliquot into single-use volumes; never refreeze a thawed aliquot.
6. Re-assay material held beyond a year if absolute concentration matters to the work.
