BPC-157 is a synthetic 15-residue peptide, sequence GEPPPGKPADDAGLV, derived from a partial sequence of a protein found in gastric juice. It is stable in aqueous solution relative to many peptides of similar length, which is one reason it appears so often in laboratory work.

This page covers what the published literature investigates, and — more usefully — where it stops. If you are evaluating this compound for a research programme, the second part matters more than the first.

## What the literature covers

Published work on BPC-157 clusters in a few areas:

- **Connective tissue models.** Tendon, ligament and muscle injury models in rodents, typically measuring repair rate, tensile properties or histological markers.
- **Gastrointestinal models.** Its origin is gastric, and a substantial portion of the early work examines gastric and intestinal lesion models.
- **Vascular and angiogenic endpoints.** Several papers examine vessel formation and blood-flow endpoints in injury models.
- **Cell-culture work.** Fibroblast migration and proliferation assays, which is where most in-vitro investigators encounter it.

A large share of this literature comes from a relatively small number of research groups. That is not a criticism of the work; it is a fact about the evidence base that anyone citing it should know, because independent replication across groups is the thing that turns a finding into a result.

## What the literature does not establish

This is the part that matters, and the part every vendor page omits:

- **There are no completed, published human clinical trials establishing efficacy for any indication.** The compound has not been approved by any major regulator for any use.
- **Rodent findings do not transfer automatically.** Exposure scaling, route and metabolism differ enough that an effect in a rat tendon model is a hypothesis about humans, not a finding about them.
- **Long-term safety data does not exist** in any population, because the studies that would produce it have not been run.
- **Mechanism is not settled.** Several pathways have been proposed in the literature; proposed is not the same as demonstrated.

If a page tells you what this compound "does" for a person, it is describing something the published record does not contain.

## What this means for sourcing material

For in-vitro work the practical questions are ordinary ones: purity by a method you can read, identity confirmed by mass spectrometry, a lot number that matches the vial, and net peptide content if your concentrations need to be accurate. The [certificate guide](/how-to-read-a-peptide-certificate-of-analysis/) covers how to check each of those.

Because the published work spans multiple groups and methods, lot-to-lot consistency matters more than usual when comparing your results against it. Reserving a single lot across a study removes one variable that is otherwise invisible in your data.

## Finding the primary literature

Search PubMed and Europe PMC for `BPC-157` and `BPC 157`, and check the reference lists of recent reviews — that is faster than working forward from individual papers. When you cite, cite the primary study rather than a review's summary of it, and note the model organism and the route in your own notes, because those are the two details most often lost when findings get repeated.
