Every vendor in this category claims high purity and third-party testing. Both claims cost nothing to make. The checks below are ordered by how much they cost you to run — the first few take minutes on the website, the last few require a conversation — and by how reliably they separate suppliers who do analytical work from suppliers who resell someone else's material with someone else's paperwork.

Apply them to every vendor you consider, including the one you already use.

## Before you contact anyone

**1. Is a certificate available before purchase?**
If the COA only arrives after payment, it cannot inform the decision. That is usually the intent. A supplier confident in their documentation publishes it on the product page.

**2. Is the certificate lot-specific, or generic?**
A certificate with no lot number, or one that appears identical across every product, describes nothing. The certificate should name the lot currently in stock, and it should change when the lot changes.

**3. Is the testing facility named?**
"Third-party tested" without a facility name is unverifiable. A named laboratory, ideally with an accreditation reference such as ISO/IEC 17025, can in principle be contacted.

**4. Is the test date visible, and recent relative to the material?**
Results describe the test date. A certificate several years older than the lot's listing date raises a reasonable question about storage history in between.

**5. Does the catalog state a specification or a result?**
"≥98%" is a promise about future lots. "99.1%, lot HX-24-0918" is a measurement. Both have a place, but a supplier who never publishes results has never shown you a measurement.

**6. Is the analytical method disclosed?**
Column, gradient, run time, detection wavelength. Without these, two suppliers' purity figures are not comparable — a faster gradient produces a higher number on identical material.

## Once you are in contact

**7. Will they provide the underlying chromatogram and spectrum?**
The summary table is a transcription. The instrument output is the evidence. A supplier who cannot produce it, or who produces an image whose sample ID does not match the lot, has answered the question.

**8. Do they report net peptide content and water content?**
Purity alone does not tell you how much peptide is in the vial. Suppliers who report net content, water content and salt form are doing analytical work beyond the minimum. Those who have never heard of the distinction are reselling.

**9. Can they reserve a lot across repeat orders?**
For any study running longer than one order cycle, lot-to-lot variation is a confound. A supplier who can hold a defined quantity of one lot for you is one who actually controls inventory rather than drop-shipping it.

**10. What happens to certificates after a lot sells out?**
Records should remain retrievable. Being able to verify a lot two years after purchase — when a reviewer, an auditor or your own reanalysis asks — is a real differentiator, and it costs a supplier nothing except discipline.

## Operational and commercial checks

**11. How is the material stored and shipped, and does the packaging match the claim?**
Ask what temperature stock is held at, whether vials are sealed and desiccated, and what the shipping configuration is. Then check the parcel against the answer when it arrives. A supplier who claims -20 °C storage and ships loose vials in a padded envelope has told you which claim to believe.

**12. Can they invoice an institution?**
Purchase orders, formal quotes, net terms and a W-9 or equivalent are unglamorous, but they indicate a real entity with accounting, and they are a practical requirement for most university and hospital purchasing. A vendor who can only take cards from individuals is a vendor with a limited operating horizon.

## Scoring in practice

Weight them. Points 1–4 are disqualifying: a supplier failing any of them is not a candidate, regardless of price. Points 5–8 separate the serious from the adequate. Points 9–12 matter most for long-running work and institutional buyers.

A useful exercise before committing to a vendor for a body of work: request the certificate, the chromatogram and the net peptide content for one specific lot, and note how long each takes to arrive and how complete it is. The response time and completeness of that one request predicts the next two years of the relationship better than any claim on the website.

## Verifying independently

For material underpinning significant work, commission an independent purity and identity check at a contract laboratory on the lot you received. The cost is usually small relative to the experimental time at risk, and it converts a supplier's claim into your own evidence. Suppliers who welcome this are the ones whose documentation holds up.

## What we hold ourselves to

The standard described above is the one this catalog is measured against: lot-specific certificates, facility named, method disclosed, records kept after a lot sells out, and institutional purchasing supported. Where a listing has no report published against it yet, that is stated on the listing rather than papered over, and the report is available on request.
