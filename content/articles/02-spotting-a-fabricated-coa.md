A certificate of analysis carries weight precisely because it is supposed to be produced by someone other than the seller. That makes it worth forging, and forged or recycled certificates are common enough in this market that every buyer should know the patterns.

None of the checks below require analytical training or specialist software. All of them can be run on a PDF in a few minutes.

## 1. The lot number does not appear on the chromatogram

A real chromatogram is generated by instrument software, and the sample identifier is printed in the data header by that software — not typed on afterwards. A certificate where the lot number appears in the document's text but nowhere in the instrument output is a document where the analytical data and the lot identity were joined by hand.

Check that the sample ID in the chromatogram header matches the lot number in the certificate body. When it doesn't, you are looking at a real chromatogram of *something*, attached to a claim about something else.

## 2. The same chromatogram appears on multiple certificates

Suppliers who fabricate documentation tend to reuse one good run. Download certificates for two different lots of the same product, or two different products, and overlay them. Peak shapes, baseline noise and retention times are effectively a fingerprint; two independent runs never look identical at the pixel level.

Identical noise patterns across two certificates mean one image was copied. Identical retention times to three decimals across lots run weeks apart mean the same thing.

## 3. The baseline is impossibly clean

A genuine RP-HPLC trace has baseline noise, some drift over the gradient, and usually small impurity peaks — even at 99%+ purity, because 99% area percent means 1% of the absorbance is something else and it has to appear somewhere.

A perfectly flat baseline with a single needle-sharp peak and nothing else is not a sign of exceptional material. It is a sign of a drawing. Look specifically for: no solvent front, no gradient drift, no minor peaks whatsoever, and a main peak whose shape is mathematically symmetrical.

## 4. Method details are missing or generic

Fabricated certificates describe results; real ones describe methods. If the document says "Purity: 99.5% (HPLC)" and stops there, there is nothing to verify.

A real method section names the column and its dimensions, the mobile phases, the gradient profile and run time, the flow rate, the injection volume and the detection wavelength. It is difficult to invent a self-consistent method section, and easy to omit one. Ask for the method; the reaction to the request is itself informative.

## 5. The mass spectrum does not match the sequence

This is the check fabricators most often fail, because it requires understanding rather than copying.

Take the sequence printed on the certificate, run it through any free peptide mass calculator, and compare the theoretical mass you get against the theoretical mass on the document. Then check that the observed ions are consistent with that mass — for ESI-MS, the multiply charged series should deconvolute correctly.

Mismatches here are not rounding. They mean the sequence, the mass, or both were transcribed from different sources.

## 6. The PDF metadata contradicts the document

Open the file's document properties. Useful fields: creation date, modification date, producing application and author.

Signals worth questioning:

- A creation date *after* the ship date of material you already received.
- A producing application that is an image editor or a word processor rather than instrument software or a lab information system.
- An author name matching the seller rather than the testing facility.
- A modification date long after creation, on a document that should never have been edited.

None of these alone proves fabrication — labs do re-export documents — but combined with anything above, the picture gets clear.

## Verifying independently

Two steps beyond the document itself:

**Contact the named laboratory.** Accredited labs will normally confirm whether a report number corresponds to work they performed. A supplier who resists you doing this is telling you something.

**Commission your own test.** For a lot you plan to rely on for a body of work, an independent purity and identity check at a contract laboratory typically costs less than the experimental time you would lose to unknowingly using the wrong material. For any long-running study, budget it as a routine cost rather than an exceptional one.

## What good documentation practice looks like from the seller's side

A supplier who is doing this properly will: publish the certificate before purchase rather than after, name the testing facility, keep certificates available after a lot sells out, provide the underlying chromatogram on request, and answer method questions without friction.
